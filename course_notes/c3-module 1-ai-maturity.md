High-Level Summary: The framework stresses that AI readiness depends on six inter‑linked dimensions—technology, data, talent, process, culture, and governance—and that an organization’s overall capability is limited by its weakest dimension. ### Overview

- This unit explains why a structured **six-dimension AI maturity assessment** produces better AI investment decisions than ad hoc readiness checks
- Key insight: **AI readiness is an organizational capability problem, not a technology problem** — and it is much harder to fix
- NASSCOM's 2024 AI Adoption Index found 87% of Indian enterprises are stuck in middle maturity tiers — not due to missing data or technology, but because four other dimensions were ignored
- Analogy used: a health checkup before a marathon — you would not test only your heart and ignore your knees, stamina, and nutrition

### The Six Dimensions of AI Maturity

- **Technology** — can the organization provision GPU compute, or is it locked into legacy infrastructure?
- **Data** — is data clean, centralized, and accessible, or fragmented across systems and spreadsheets?
- **Talent** — do people have AI literacy sufficient to evaluate vendor proposals and manage deployments?
- **Process** — are workflows standardized and documented, or manual and inconsistent?
- **Culture** — does middle management see AI as a productivity tool or a headcount threat?
- **Governance** — are there data privacy policies, an AI ethics framework, and clear ownership of AI decisions?
- **Critical rule: your actual readiness is capped by your weakest dimension**, not your strongest

### Case Study 1 — Rohit Sharma (Large Indian PSU)

- Rohit, Director of Transformation, brought an AI investment proposal to the board — it was stopped by the question: *"How do we know we are ready for this?"*
- Assessment revealed: one strong dimension, four weak, one mixed
    - **Technology** — 15-year-old ERP with hard-coded integrations blocked real-time data feeds
    - **Data** — customer data was clean, but supply chain data was spread across 47 spreadsheets in regional offices
    - **Talent** — strong domain experts with zero AI literacy
    - **Process** — procurement workflows were manual, undocumented, and inconsistent across plants
    - **Culture** — middle management viewed AI as a headcount threat
    - **Governance** — no data privacy policy, no AI ethics framework, no ownership of AI decisions (disqualifying for a regulated PSU)
- **Resolution**: Rohit built a sequenced roadmap — fix governance and data gaps in Q1, run talent upskilling in parallel, then launch a single pilot in customer service (the function where all six dimensions aligned best)
- The board approved the revised plan

### Case Study 2 — Ritu Kapoor (Mid-Sized Indian Private Bank)

- Ritu, head of finance, pushed for an AI-powered fraud detection system with solid technology and clean data
- The project was shelved after six months because:
    - The compliance team had no framework to validate AI-driven decisions (**governance gap**)
    - Branch managers actively overrode system alerts due to distrust (**culture gap**)
- Lesson: the two dimensions nobody assessed were the ones that killed the project

### Key Takeaways

- Most leaders only assess technology and data — this is the wrong approach
- Deloitte labels organizations with lots of technology but no organizational muscle as *"starters"* — the framework catches this before money is spent
- The six-dimension framework turns a tool-buying meeting into a **strategy conversation** by asking: *where are we strong, where are we exposed, and what has to be true before this succeeds?*
- The shift in mindset: from *"what AI should we buy?"* to *"where are we actually ready?"*

- This video focuses on the **first dimension: Technology Readiness** — defined as an organization's ability to provision compute, store and serve data, and connect AI models to where actual work happens
- Technology readiness rests on **three pillars: Infrastructure, Integration, and Technical Debt**

### The Three Pillars

- **Infrastructure**: Ability to provision GPU compute and scalable storage on demand; cloud is necessary but not sufficient
- **Integration Layer**: Whether AI models can connect to ERP, CRM, and core systems through clean APIs; described as *the most critical pillar*
- **Technical Debt**: Legacy code, hardware logic, and years of customization that create data inconsistency even when integration exists

### Case Study: Ananya Mehta, VP Operations (FMCG Company)

- Goal: AI-powered demand forecasting to predict regional demand 48 hours ahead, shifting production planning from monthly to weekly cycles
- Budget was approved, vendor locked, data science team hired — yet the project stalled six months in
- **Infrastructure wall**: Data lived on-premise without GPU compute; IT policy had not been updated to allow cloud-scale compute for AI workloads
- **Integration wall** (most critical): SAP, Salesforce, and a custom warehouse management system had no APIs; data flowed manually via weekly spreadsheets — incompatible with a model needing daily data
- **Technical debt wall**: SAP carried 14 years of customization with hard-coded regional logic across product codes, pricing, and discounts, making data inconsistent even if integration were solved

### How Ananya Fixed It

- Prioritized integration as the bottleneck blocking everything downstream
- Built an **API-first middleware platform** as a translation layer between SAP, Salesforce, and the AI model
- While integration was being built, the team cleaned data inconsistencies; compute was ultimately the easiest fix
- **Result**: Four months in, integration was live in two regions; the demand model moved from lab prototype to production pilot; forecast accuracy improved **31%** vs. the old Excel process

### Key Takeaways

- Integration is the pillar that most commonly kills AI projects — it is the bridge between infrastructure and technical debt; without it, a model has no data and no system to act on
- If data flows involve manual extracts or Excel uploads, the organization has an integration gap
- A contrasting example: a large Indian private bank invested in an API-first integration platform **two years before** their first AI project; when they launched an AI-driven credit underwriting model, pilot-to-production took under three months
- Technology readiness is not about the latest tools — it is about connections

### Diagnostic Questions to Ask in Technology Review Meetings

- Can we provision AI compute on demand?
- Can our core systems feed data to an AI model through clean APIs?
- How much legacy customization stands in the way?
- For our highest-priority AI use case, how many systems need to connect — and how many of those connections exist as clean APIs today?

### Overview

- This video is part of a series on AI maturity; it focuses on **data readiness**, the second pillar after technology readiness
- The integration layer — which data readiness underpins — is where most AI projects stall
- NASSCOM research shows 40% of Indian organizations struggle not with data volume, but with **siloed data, inconsistent formats, and absent governance**
- The core question leaders must shift to: not *"Do we have data?"* but *"Do we have the right data?"*

### The Three Tests Every Data Asset Must Pass

- **Quality** — Is the data accurate, complete, consistent, and timely?
- **Accessibility** — Can the right systems access data directly, without manual extraction or human intervention?
- **Strategic Value** — Does the data contain the signals actually needed for the AI outcome being targeted, or was it collected for a different purpose (e.g., compliance)?
- Strategic value is the most commonly skipped test, yet it is what makes data *sufficient* rather than just *necessary*
- Clean, accessible data that lacks the right signals will produce a model that is **confidently wrong**

### Case Study 1: Shalini Gupta — Financial Forecasting

- **Goal:** Rolling 12-week AI-powered revenue forecast for a D2C brand, to be live in one quarter
- **Quality gaps:** Marketing spend data had a 23% gap; Instagram and YouTube campaigns were tracked at brand level only, not SKU level — making it impossible to link spend to specific products
- **Accessibility gaps:** Transaction data was in SAP; marketing data was spread across Google Analytics and three agency dashboards; product catalogs were Excel files last updated four months prior — none connected to each other
- **Strategic value gaps:** Data was collected for accounting, not prediction; no competitor data, no seasonal tags
- **Smart resolution:** Rather than declaring the project non-viable or launching with poor data, Shalini scoped the model to the **one data slice that passed all three tests** — online D2C transactions — with clean data, API accessibility, and meaningful pricing and demand signals
- **Result:** The narrow model outperformed the finance team's manual forecast by **19% in the first eight weeks**, giving Shalini evidence to secure board approval for broader data infrastructure investment

### Case Study 2: Hospital Chain — Patient No-Show Prediction

- **Setup:** AI model built on three years of appointment data to predict patient no-shows; data was clean and accessible; initial accuracy was **70%**
- **Strategic value gap:** The model did not account for whether a patient received a WhatsApp reminder — a signal highly relevant in Indian healthcare
- **Fix:** Adding the reminder signal raised accuracy to **90%**
- **Lesson:** The failure was not quality or accessibility — it was a missing signal, i.e., a strategic value gap

### Key Takeaways

- Replace the question *"How much data do we have?"* with three targeted questions :
    - Is it clean enough to trust?
    - Can systems access it without human intervention?
    - Does it contain the signals driving the outcome we want?
- When a team member says *"we have most of it,"* probe further — ask which signals are missing; that is where the real gap lies
- A scoped, high-quality pilot on the right data slice can build the internal case for broader data investment

- Talent readiness is the dimension that ties technology and data together — without it, infrastructure and data cannot generate value
- A common failure pattern in Indian enterprises: hire one strong external expert, project succeeds while they are present, everything stops when they leave
- **Talent readiness is not about hiring one smart person** — it is about building capability across the organization

### Case Study: Meena (Asha Roy), Operations Director, Indian Logistics Company

- Tasked with building an AI-powered route optimization system; budget was approved and a consultant was hired
- Consultant left after six months, freezing the project — not because the model was bad, but because no one else in the organization could engage with it
- **Three gaps revealed:** no mid-level talent to translate technical work into business decisions ; leadership treated AI as something the consultant owned ; middle management saw the system as a threat to their scheduling authority

### The Three Pillars of Talent Readiness

- **Skills inventory:** Requires a pyramid of skills — data scientists and engineers at the top, but also AI literacy at the business leader, product manager, and analyst levels; most organizations have one or two technical people and widespread dependency elsewhere
- **Learning culture:** Ability to upskill existing talent at speed; resistance is common, especially when employees associate AI automation with job loss; passive non-engagement (not outright refusal) is the typical symptom
- **Leadership capability:** Leaders must think *strategically* about AI, not just delegate it technically; Meena's CEO viewed AI as a cost center rather than a capability to build, which shaped investment decisions
- A gap in any single pillar creates a bottleneck the others cannot compensate for

### Meena's Recovery: A Sequenced Talent-Building Approach

- **Month 1:** Rapid upskilling for key middle managers — enough to understand the route optimization model and its limitations
- **Month 2:** One-day AI strategy workshop for the leadership team (not focused on technical details)
- Hired a second data scientist for continuity, not as a consultant replacement
- Within four months: shifted from "the consultant owns this" to "we own this"; model moved from frozen to live in production
- Used the external consultant engagement as an opportunity to build internal talent in parallel

### Counter-Example: Large Indian Bank

### The Three Pillars of Process Readiness

- **Standardization**: AI models assume consistent inputs; when the same action is captured differently across teams, the model treats them as different things

- **Automation baseline**: Organizations cannot jump directly from manual processes to AI; basic rule-based automation must be established first as a foundation

### Case Study 1: Pharma Territory Optimization (Aditya Krishnan)
