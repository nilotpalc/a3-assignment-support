### The 5-Source Framework Overview

Each source operates on a different time horizon and reveals a different layer of competitive intent:   

| Source | What it reveals | Time horizon | Key signal to read |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Patent filings | Where competitors invest in AI R&D (the blueprints) | Typically 12–18 months ahead of product launches | Shift from foundational to application-specific patents |
| Job postings | Capabilities being built right now (the workforce) | Present / near term | Role seniority and reporting lines |
| Product announcements | What is deployed today (the finished building) | Today | AI as core value proposition with measurable customer outcomes vs. cosmetic feature |
| Corporate & regulatory filings | Budget commitments and risk assessments (the budget) | Quarterly / annual reporting cycles | Separate AI CapEx line item; board oversight disclosure |
| CI platforms | Aggregated, structured view of acquisitions and funding flows (the view from above) | Often ahead of filings and press releases by quarters | Acquisition signals and funding flows |

### Key Mental Model

- AI does not uniformly intensify competition — it **changes which forces dominate**
- Think of the 5 Forces as 5 walls: AI weakens some, strengthens others, and builds entirely new walls where none existed
- Common mistake: assuming AI primarily cranks up competitive rivalry; the real shift is structural
- Data flywheels are central: more users → more data → better models → more users, creating self-reinforcing barriers

### AI's Impact on Each Force (Indian Industry Examples)

- **Force 1 — Barriers to Entry**: AI simultaneously lowers and raises barriers
    - *Lowers*: Fintechs can use cloud infrastructure and alternative data for credit scoring without branch networks
    - *Raises*: Data flywheels create new moats (e.g., Google's search infrastructure)
    - Indian IT services (~60% domestic market share) face AI-native firms competing on outcomes with fewer people
- **Force 2 — Buyer Power**: AI increases buyer power through easier comparison and switching, but firms can counteract via AI-driven personalization and platform lock-in
- **Force 3 — Threat of Substitutes**: Significant disruption in insurance
    - Acko and Digit use fully digital underwriting and AI-automated claims, reducing turnaround from days to minutes
    - Digit reports 75% reduction in claims processing time, resetting customer expectations
- **Force 4 — Supplier Power**: AI-powered logistics platforms (e.g., Delhivery) use route optimization to reduce dependence on any single supplier
- **Force 5 — Competitive Rivalry**: In retail/CPG, AI enables demand forecasting, dynamic pricing, and personalized recommendations, raising the minimum viable competitive standard

| **Porter's Five Force** | **How AI reshapes the force** |
| --- | --- |
| Barriers to entry | AI lowers entry barriers by enabling AI-native businesses to scale with limited physical infrastructure, whereas data flywheels create new competitive barriers for established organisations. |
| Buyer power | AI enables customers to compare alternatives more easily, whereas AI-driven personalisation helps organisations strengthen customer relationships and reduce switching. |
| Threat of substitutes | AI-native products and services create new alternatives that redefine customer expectations and challenge traditional business models. |
| Supplier power | AI-powered logistics platforms can improve coordination and optimisation across supply chains, reducing dependence on individual suppliers in some contexts. |
| Competitive rivalry | AI raises the minimum competitive standard through capabilities such as demand forecasting, dynamic pricing and personalised customer experiences. |

### Overview

- This is an educational lecture on how to decide whether to pursue a **first mover** or **fast follower** AI strategy, built around a three-factor decision framework
- The framework is illustrated through Meena Rajan, a CHRO whose CEO challenged her leadership team: *"Are we building our own data advantage or planning to catch up later?"*
- Key insight: neither strategy is universally superior — the right choice must be made **initiative by initiative**, not at the company level

### The Core Framework: Three Decision Factors

- **Factor 1 — Data Moat Potential:** Does this initiative create a proprietary data asset that compounds over time? If yes → **first mover**. If the data is widely available and models are off-the-shelf → **fast follower**
- **Factor 2 — Regulatory Risk:** Is the regulatory landscape clear enough to deploy without existential compliance risk? If no → **fast follower** until governance matures
- **Factor 3 — Organizational Readiness:** Does the organization have the talent, infrastructure, and culture to execute at production quality? If no → **fast follower** while building capabilities

### When First Mover Wins: Data Flywheels

- First mover advantage is strongest where **data creates compounding advantages** — every new user or transaction improves the model
- **Tesla:** 5M vehicles generating ~50B miles of driving data/year — a dataset competitors with smaller fleets cannot replicate; over-the-air updates create a compound interest dynamic
- **Google:** Processes billions of queries daily; its crawler sees 3–5x more web content than some AI competitors, powering Gemini AI
- **Reliance Jio:** ~4–5 petabytes of data/day from hundreds of millions of subscribers; building Jio Brain AI platform as a domestic data moat
- **Swiggy & Zomato:** Three-sided networks with dynamic routing algorithms that improve with every transaction

### When Fast Follower Wins: Maturing Tech & High Risk

- Fast follower advantage is strongest where **technology is maturing rapidly** and early adoption risks are high
- **Amazon's cautionary tale:** Spent three years on an AI hiring algorithm that was scrapped for systematically discriminating against women — a reputational lesson fast followers learned for free
- **ACKO & Digit (Indian insurance):** Entered after cloud, ML, and API ecosystems matured; leapfrogged incumbents on cost and customer experience by building digital underwriting from scratch using proven tech
- **Indian banking:** McKinsey notes incumbents can close the gap by scanning fintech innovations and industrializing proven use cases without building core infrastructure from scratch
- **NASSCOM finding:** Only a fraction of Indian firms experimenting with AI achieve scaled deployment — a disciplined fast-follower approach focused on scaling proven use cases may outperform scattered first-mover bets

### Key Frameworks & Mental Models

- Brynjolfsson and McAfee: *"The bottleneck in AI is not algorithms — it's management imagination and implementation."* Disciplined adopters can catch up by focusing on process redesign and experimentation
- **Hybrid strategies are the mark of success:** Tesla is a first mover on driving data but a fast follower on manufacturing robotics
- **TCS example:** Upskilled 300,000+ employees on Gen AI; over a third of client engagements incorporate AI — a first mover bet on talent as a data advantage

### Core Concepts

- **Data Flywheel**: A self-reinforcing cycle — more users → more data → better AI models → better products → more users
- **Network Effect**: Each additional user makes the product more valuable for all existing users, amplifying the flywheel
- **Proprietary Data Moat**: Data collected through operations that is unique, difficult to replicate, and serves as the primary input for AI models competitors cannot match
- **Compound Data Advantage**: The widening performance gap over time — not from better algorithms, but from richer, more diverse data with every interaction
- Analogy: Like **compound interest** — an early data deposit earns returns (improved model performance), which attracts more usage, which generates larger deposits; a new entrant starting from zero cannot catch up in a reasonable timeframe

### Why Data Volume Alone Is Not Enough

- Common misconception: more data automatically means better AI
- Reality: once a model trains on sufficiently large data, **marginal gains from additional volume diminish**
- The real moat comes from data that is **proprietary, continuously refreshed, and tightly integrated into a feedback loop** that competitors cannot replicate

### Three-Layer Mental Model for a Defensible Data Moat

1. **Unique data generation** — collecting data nobody else can access 
2. **Closed-loop learning** — AI models improve from that data and push better outputs back to users 
3. **Switching costs** — as users rely on an improving AI, leaving becomes increasingly costly 

### Global Examples

- **Tesla**: 5M+ vehicles generate ~50 billion miles of real-world driving data per year; closed-loop architecture flags novel scenarios fleet-wide, retrains models, and pushes updates over-the-air to all vehicles — every mile improves every car; competitors face a compounding disadvantage from slower feedback loops
- **Google**: Processes billions of daily queries; Gemini models estimated to have 3–5× more content than some AI competitors; user behavior (clicks, dwell time, reformulations) continuously improves search and AI models

### Indian Examples

- **Reliance Jio**: Hundreds of millions of subscribers; generates ~4–5 petabytes/day of network, usage, and behavioral data; uses AI for network optimization, fault management, and personalization; building **GeoBrain** as a horizontal AI platform to convert telecom data into an enterprise AI service
- **Swiggy / Zomato**: Three-sided network (consumers, restaurants, delivery fleets) generating data on orders, locations, timings, pricing, and delivery performance; AI used for dynamic routing, demand prediction, rider allocation, and restaurant analytics; moat is primarily from **network effects** — replicating all three sides simultaneously is extraordinarily difficult
- Key lesson from Jio: AI can be built on **operational data not originally designed for AI** — data quality concerns should not be a barrier to starting

### The Three-Question Evaluation Framework

- Does your organization generate **proprietary data** that competitors cannot easily obtain?
- Is that data being fed into AI models through a **closed-loop system** that improves with every interaction?
- Do your users face **increasing switching costs** as your AI improves?
- If all three are yes → defensible data moat; if any is no → that is exactly where to invest

### Limitations and Risks to Data Moats

- **Antitrust regulation**: Regulators increasingly target data advantages; incumbents may be required to share data with competitors
- **Data localization and privacy laws**: India's DPDP Act, consent requirements, and restrictions on sensitive data can limit how firms exploit data moats
- **Technological challengers**: Advances in synthetic data, transfer learning, and specialized models may allow challengers to compete effectively **without matching incumbents' raw data volumes**, especially in niche domains
- Conclusion: A data moat is powerful but **not permanent** — it requires continuous investment, governance, and adaptation

### The Three Task Categories

| Task Category | Definition | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Automatable** | Tasks with clear inputs, rules and expected outputs that machines can execute reliably | Data validation, invoice reconciliation and meeting scheduling |
| **Augmentable** | Tasks where AI improves human performance while the human remains responsible for the work | Writing, customer service and medical diagnosis support |
| **Irreducibly human** | Tasks where judgement, context or relationships are essential and cannot be fully delegated to AI | Negotiation, firing decisions and identifying systemic customer issues |

### The 3-Step Task Triage Framework

- **Step 1 — Decompose:** Break a single role into 30–50 discrete, granular tasks (not broad activities like "analyze data," but specific steps like "pull customer spending from CRM" or "reconcile against data warehouse")
    - Granularity is critical because automatability depends entirely on specificity
- **Step 2 — Classify:** For each task, ask three questions
    - Is there a deterministic rule set a machine could execute consistently?
    - Does the task require context from outside the immediate workflow? If yes → likely augmentable, not automatable
    - Would the organization be comfortable with a machine making this decision without human review? If no → not automatable
- **Step 3 — Redesign:** For each automatable task, the key question is where to **redeploy** the freed capacity
    - ❌ Worst outcome: eliminate the task → reduce headcount
    - ✅ Better outcome: eliminate the task → redeploy capacity into augmentable and irreducibly human work
- **Typical task distribution** across most roles: 15–20% automatable, 30–40% augmentable, 20–30% irreducibly human

### Case Study: Priya Kumar's Business Analyst Team (Positive Example)

- Priya Kumar manages a team of business analysts at a large Indian financial services firm

- After redesign: data gathering dropped from 40% → 10%; analysts now spend 60% of time on synthesis and interpretation
- Result: **output per analyst tripled** with the same headcount
- Key reframe: instead of asking *"can we reduce headcount?"* she asked *"what complex synthesis work can analysts finally have time for?"*

### The Three Frameworks

- **Deloitte's Principle-Based Approach**: Design organizational structure around 5 non-negotiable principles for how AI should work
    - Example principles: all AI decisions must be explainable to regulators (requiring an embedded ethics officer), or AI should not replace human judgment (requiring human-in-the-loop implementations)
    - **Strength**: Flexibility and principle-first design
    - **Weakness**: Requires clarity on principles before designing structure — many organizations lack this clarity
- **MIT's Sociotechnical Model**: Assumes every AI implementation has two systems — a technical system (algorithms, infrastructure, models) and a social system (people, workflows, governance) — and insists both must be designed together
    - Requires thinking about how AI changes power structures, incentives, and relationships
    - Example: deploying AI that removes decision-making from middle managers changes their incentive structure; if their role isn't redesigned, they will resist the system
    - **Strength**: Forces social thinking alongside technical design
    - **Weakness**: Complex, slow, and requires deep stakeholder involvement — not a top-down redesign
- **Accenture's Transition Stage Model**: Stages the transformation for large organizations that can't leap from no AI to fully integrated AI at once
    - Stage 1: Establish AI Centers of Excellence — islands of expertise to scale from pilots to production
    - Stage 2: Introduce AI governance — shared standards, models, and data across Centers of Excellence
    - Stage 3: Enable organization-wide AI — integrate AI across functions so legacy and AI roles work together in hybrid teams
    - **Strength**: Pragmatic; acknowledges large organizations move slowly
    - **Weakness**: Can lock organizations into a Center of Excellence model that creates new silos rather than breaking them down

### Choosing the Right Framework by Maturity

- **Early adoption** (0–2 AI teams, no clear governance): Start with **Accenture's Transition Stage Model** — jumping to MIT or Deloitte requires buy-in and principle clarity that don't yet exist
- **Scaling** (3+ functions, integration issues emerging): Transition to **MIT's Sociotechnical Model**, applied gradually function by function
- **Mature** (multiple AI teams, clear governance, scaling for agility): Apply **Deloitte's Principle-Based Approach** — sufficient clarity exists to design structure around principles

### The Jagged Technological Frontier (JACT)

- The JACT describes which tasks AI currently performs better than humans and which tasks humans still lead — the boundary is **jagged, not smooth**
- AI excels at: pattern matching, simulations, processing text at scale
- AI underperforms on: common sense reasoning, contextual understanding, human relationship dynamics
- The frontier shifts over time as AI capabilities improve
- **Practical implication:** The gap between human and AI capability on a given task indicates how much a collaborative design can improve performance

    - Officers treated the AI score as a final answer rather than an input to judgment
    - Default rates rose because the human judgment layer was lost without improving decision quality
